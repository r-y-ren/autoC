## I. INTRODUCTION

customersâ doorsteps has increased due to the expansion of urban areas. Traditional ground transportation faces challenges in ensuring seamless delivery operations for e-commerce companies due to growing road congestion. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) or drones have the potential to deliver packages more efficiently than traditional transportation [1]. These drones efficiently maneuver over various terrains to navigate their route in the airspace to ensure ubiquitous and prompt deliveries [2].

Existing work on drone-based delivery services leverages the service paradigm to model Drone-as-a-Service (DaaS). A drone service is defined in terms of its functional and non-functional attributes [3]. The functional attribute of a drone service refers to the transportation of a package between two nodes [4]. The non-functional attributes are the Quality of Service (QoS) parameters such as energy consumption, cost, and delivery time. Drones associated with these services operate within a skyway network [4]. A skyway network consists of an interconnected set of nodes, serving as the endpoints of skyway segments (Fig. 1(a)). The skyway segment is a Line of Sight (LoS) path between the nodes. The nodes are the building rooftops that may serve as both charging stations and delivery destinations for drones. The skyway network restricts drone entry in specific areas, defined based on airspace safety regulations.1 These areas include No-Fly-Zones (NFZs), and Temporary-No-Fly-Zones (TNFZs).

Recent research focuses on drone service composition to ensure on-time package delivery [4]. The proposed approaches optimize the composition of drone services based on specific QoS requirements. These drone services refer to an optimal set of drone-operated segments. However, a key assumption in existing solutions is that drones operate smoothly without interference from other drones. This assumption is unrealistic, as the expected growth in drone traffic will result in drones flying in close proximity [5]. Drones traveling closely may result in drones impacting each other [6]. For example, aerodynamic forces between closely flying drones may increase their energy consumption. This interference may cause dronesâ longer charging times, impacting the expected QoS requirements, e.g., delivery time.

Airbusâs recent study forecasts 16,000 drone flights per hour in Paris by 2035, within an area of 105.4 km2[7]. This density translates to 79 simultaneous flights per square kilometer, with each flight lasting about 30 minutes. As large drones (e.g., DJI Flycart 30, Amazon MK30, EHangFalcon) carrying payloads of up to 10 kg are expected to operate within delimited airspace, such high traffic density will inevitably result in drones operating in close-proximity within shared skyway segments [5], [8]. Furthermore, this likelihood increases as each provider plans routes independently and selfishly, often prioritizing the shortest paths for efficiency. This may result in multiple drones traversing the same segment in close proximity, potentially impacting drone performance due to aerodynamic turbulence generated by nearby drones [9]. For instance, a drone may consume more battery power than initially anticipated to counteract turbulence. This increase in battery power consumption occurs as the droneâs motors draw extra power to rotate the propellers faster to maintain stability within the turbulent region. This may cause dronesâ longer charging times or the risk of a mid-air emergency landing due to excessive battery depletion, impacting the expected QoS requirements (e.g., delivery time, safety). Therefore, this significant increase in drone traffic demands that the DaaS skyway infrastructure adapts to ensure the smooth operation of drones.

An end-to-end Service Oriented Architecture (SOA) is required to orchestrate smooth drone operations in the skyway network. We identify three main components of a drone-based SOA: service-provider, service-consumer, and super-provider. A service-provider2 is the drone owner who offers delivery services. Service-consumers are the end customers who invoke drone services from service providers based on their delivery requirements. A super-provider manages the skyway network to ensure the smooth operation of drones.

Smoothness refers to ensuring the resilient operation of drone services. A resilient drone service adapts to the failures occurring in the skyway network. We define failure as an event that impacts the safety and efficiency of drone services. These failures may result in collisions or near collisions (a.k.a near misses). A collision occurs when drones come in direct contact, compromising safety. For instance, physical damage may occur when two drones traveling in opposite directions converge at the same altitude in the same skyway segment. In contrast, nearcollision affects the efficiency of drones when they operate in close spatio-temporal proximity. For instance, the aerodynamic interference between nearby drones may increase their energy consumption [6]. As a result, drones may deviate from their expected trajectory. This deviation may cause a risk of damage or a later arrival than their scheduled delivery time. Hence, addressing failures that may impact the safety or efficiency of drones is crucial to ensure the smooth operation of drones. Existing research focuses on the detection of collisions that impact drones safety [10]. In this work, we focus on the failures that may impact the efficiency of drones.

Failures occur due to inter-drone interference. We define interference as any spatio-temporal event that hinders the efficient operation of drones. In the context of service-based drone deliveries, we propose a novel drone service interference taxonomy to classify various types of interference that may occur between drone services. Interference may occur at the node-level or skyway segment-level as shown in Fig. 1. A node-level interference is defined as any event that delays the provisioning of dronesâ recharging requirements at the nodes. For example, node-level interference may occur when there are insufficient recharging pads to accommodate multiple dronesâ access requests (Fig. 1(c)). A segment-level interference refers to an event that impacts the expected segment-level requirements of drones (e.g., energy consumption). This interference may occur due to a spatio-temporal overlap in the trajectories of drones operating in the same segment. As a result, the expected energy consumption of drones is influenced either positively or negatively. For example, a positive segment-level interference may occur when the drone consumes less battery than anticipated due to upwash forces generated by a drone flying below it (Fig. 1(c)). In contrast, a negative segment-level interference may occur when a drone consumes more battery than initially expected due to the downwash forces from another drone situated above it (Fig. 1(c)).

There may be instances where segment-level interference may lead to node-level interference. For instance, increased energy consumption due to segment-level interferences may cause multiple drones to arrive at the subsequent nodes to recharge for a longer duration than initially planned. This may result in a congestion buildup at the node, causing the drones to wait for their turn to occupy the recharging pads. In this paper, we focus on the proactive detection of negative segment-level inter-drone interference. This detection serves as a foundation for effective interference resolution strategies by mitigating the propagation of interferences to the node-level for resilient drone servicesâ operation. External factors such as GPS jamming, spoofing, thunderstorm sounds, and birds may disrupt the data reception and transmission between drones. As a result, it may threaten the accurate detection of inter-drone interferences. However, addressing these factors is out of the scope of this paper. The prior research studies have addressed the detection and resolution of these types of interference [11]. A key aspect of resolving segment-level interferences is assessing the severity of interference on the expected QoS requirements of drone services. Some interferences are critical and require immediate resolution, while others may be negligible. For instance, a drone may consume more battery due to a long duration of spatio-temporal overlap with the peer drone. As a result, it may arrive late at the destination due to an increase in recharging time at the intermediate node. However, the impact of some interference events may be negligible. For instance, the expected battery consumption of drones may get negligibly impacted if drones operate in close proximity for a few seconds. In this case, drones may arrive at their scheduled destination with a delay of a few minutes. Therefore, it is crucial to assess the severity level to devise effective interference resolution strategies.

Detecting and assessing the severity of inter-drone interferences is challenging due to the dynamic spatio-temporal requirements of multiple drone providers using the skyway network. For example, each provider may operate drones at different altitudes and times across multiple segments to meet specific QoS requirements. Additionally, the impact of interference may vary based on the spatial proximity and spatial configuration of drones operating in the same segments. For instance, drones with a vertical separation of 30 cm may experience significantly greater interference than drones 80 cm apart. Another example, a drone operating in a downwash region may be significantly more affected compared to a drone operating in an upwash region. To address the aforementioned challenges we propose a novel segment-level interference detection framework to proactively detect and assess the severity level of inter-drone interferences in a skyway segment. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

- Novel Inter-drone Interference Detection Framework: We propose a service-based framework to monitor and assess inter-drone interference in skyway networks. It detects interferences by analyzing spatio-temporal dynamics in 3D space and evaluates their severity for effective mitigation.

. Comprehensive Interference Taxonomy: Our taxonomy categorizes interferences into node-level (e.g., recharging pad congestion) and segment-level (e.g., aerodynamic interactions). This classification helps devise tailored strategies for addressing interferences based on their type and context.

Segment-level Interference Detection Algorithm: We developed a heuristic-based algorithm to detect interference events in 3D space efficiently. It uses real data to evaluate spatio-temporal proximity and performs faster than baseline methods.

Segment-level Interference Severity Assessment Method: We introduce a severity assessment using the Percentage Impact Score (PIS) metric. This method quantifies the impact of interferences on energy consumption and delivery times. This enables prioritized and effective mitigation measures to address critical interferences.

The current approach for inter-drone interference detection assumes that the super-provider is aware of the drone trajectories. This assumption would be relaxed in future work as uncertainties in weather conditions and the trustworthiness of drone providers may compromise the completeness of superprovidersâ knowledge and, consequently, the accuracy of preflight inter-drone interference detection. For instance, a drone provider may intentionally extend their droneâs use of a recharging pad during peak demand. Similarly, drones may deviate from their planned path due to unfavorable weather conditions during flight operations. Future work will address these factors to allow dynamic interference detection during real-time drone operations.

## II. MOTIVATING SCENARIO

Let us consider a scenario where multiple drone providers operate single drones in a skyway network. Each drone provider composes an optimal set of drone services to deliver packages from the source node to the destination node [4]. We assume that drone providers comply with the regulatory practices established by the Civil Aviation Safety Authority (CASA). In this regard, they submit detailed flight plans to the superprovider to ensure safe airspace utilization [12]. Therefore, the super-provider is aware of the drone trajectories and recharging duration at the nodes. The drone servicesâ expected QoS requirements must be fulfilled to ensure the package delivery on the scheduled time. However, the expectations of drone providers may not be fulfilled due to inter-drone interferences occurring in segments. As a result, drones may arrive later than initially anticipated by the drone providers. ## A. Service-Based Drone Deliveries

Logistic companies are globally transforming their business models to provide drone-based delivery services [13]. There has been a growing interest in using the service paradigm for composing the right set of drone services to ensure the package delivery from source to destination node based on QoS parameters. The recent work in service-based drone deliveries proposed an algorithm for optimal drone service selection and composition [14]. However, the drone service environment is highly dynamic due to uncertain weather conditions. This uncertainty may lead to the unavailability of recharging pads at nodes, thus leading to a delayed package delivery. A resilient DaaS composition framework is proposed considering the uncertainty in the availability of recharging pads due to weather conditions [15]. However, the proposed framework does not consider the uncertainty of wind conditions in skyway segments. A drone service composition approach is proposed considering the probability of wind speed and direction in each skyway segment using the Probabilistic Forward Search (PFS) algorithm [16] as the uncertainty in wind speed and direction impacts the optimal composition of drone services.

Recent work also focuses on using a swarm of drones for package deliveries. A swarm-based drone for package delivery refers to a set of drones that operate together from a source to a destination node within time and spatial constraints [17]. A swarm of drones leverages faster and more efficient package delivery by alleviating the limitations (e.g., limited payload capacity) associated with single drone-based package delivery [6]. A novel swarm-based drones-as-a-service (SDaaS) model is proposed that leverages cooperative behavior among drones to reduce the waiting time associated with the sequential charging at nodes [18]. However, the proposed approach does not consider the dynamic wind conditions inherent to a drone service environment. A study is conducted for different types of drone formations and their impact on energy consumption in varying wind conditions [6]. The proposed algorithm informs optimal drone formation, ensuring the least energy consumption in the segments. Unlike single-drone-based package delivery, swarmbased package delivery requires multiple drones to recharge at intermediate nodes. The drones may recharge fully or partially at intermediate nodes depending on the drone servicesâ requirements. However, the skyway network is a constrained space with limited recharging pads. An in-flight energy-sharing framework is proposed for swarm-based drone services considering delivery time requirements [19]. The proposed framework enables the optimal set of support drones to share energy among drones. As a result, the energy sharing minimizes the probability of drone swarm waiting or stopping at intermediate nodes for recharging. The experimental results show that the priority-based energy sharing methods perform better in delivering packages successfully to the destinations.

These single-drone and swarm-based drone delivery services compete for airspace usage to fulfill their expected requirements in terms of service composition. This increasing competition may lead to interference between dronesâ operations, thus impacting their expected QoS requirements. To the best of our knowledge, no existing studies on service-based drone deliveries have examined the impact of inter-drone interferences on drone service composition. Hence, this work is the first attempt to monitor the usage of skyway segments to detect and assess the impact of interferences on drone servicesâ efficiency.

## B. Conflict Detection in UAV Traffic Management

FAA and NASA introduced the Unmanned Traffic Management (UTM) framework for maintaining safe separation between drones, given the increasing volume of drone traffic [20]. This framework manages drone flights in compliance with air traffic regulations. Research in UAV traffic management mainly focuses on maintaining the conflict-free operation of drones in shared airspace. The current UTM models define conflict as any event that results in collision due to loss of safe separation between drones [10]. Several UTM models are proposed to mitigate UAV conflicts through conflict detection and resolution. Conflict detection identifies possible loss of safe separation between UAVs, leading to safety hazards such as collisions. In contrast, conflict resolution refers to resolving conflicts to ensure safe separation between UAVs.

Recent literature presents deterministic approaches for conflict detection among UAVs [21], [22], [10]. The work in [21] identifies the conflicting paths between flight pairs. In this respect, they determine the conflicting regions as overlapping paths between two UAVs. A state-based conflict detection strategy is proposed based on the extrapolation of dronesâ trajectories [22]. These extrapolated trajectories indicate the breach in safe separation between drones. A dynamic conflict detection framework is proposed based on trajectory extrapolation through historical observation data of UAVs [23]. The proposed framework leverages the Interacting Multi-Model (IMM) for trajectory tracking. The proposed approach uses the Gilbertâ JohnsonâKeerthi (GJK) algorithm to overcome the issue of low computational efficiency in traditional conflict detection approaches. A collision detection approach is proposed based on the collision cone concept. The proposed approach identifies a collision event by analyzing the position, velocity, and heading of both the obstacle and the UAV.

Existing drone-based frameworks focus on detecting potential collisions between drones. To the best of our knowledge, there is no work on detecting interferences that impact the efficient operation of drones in the skyway network. This work is the first attempt to develop an inter-drone interference detection framework in the context of service-based drone deliveries. This framework will serve as a pre-requisite to ensure resilient operation of drone services.

## A. Drone Services Interference Taxonomy

In the context of drone operations, an interference refers to the disruption in the transmission and reception of data between drones and control systems. However, we are defining interference as an event that hinders the efficient operation of drones in a skyway network. This hindrance affects the fulfillment of expected QoS requirements for drone services. We present a novel drone service interference taxonomy (Fig. 5). We categorize the interference into two main levels based on the location of occurrence. The first level specifies the location (i.e., node or skyway segment) of interference within the skyway network. The second level outlines different types of interference at both the node and segment levels. The node-level interference is out of the scope of this paper and we are mentioning it here as a part of the proposed taxonomy. Below we describe each type in detail:

- Node-level Interference: This interference refers to any event that delays the provisioning of dronesâ recharging requirements at a node resulting in late package delivery. We assume that this delay manifests in the form of congestion at the node-level. We formally define a node-level interference (N LI) as follows:

$$
N L I = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { 1 } & { i f , \frac { \lambda } { R _ { n u m b } * \mu } > 1 } \\ { 0 } & { \mathrm { o t h e r w i s e } } \end{array} \right.\tag{8}
$$

where Î» and Î¼ are the arrival rate and service rate4 of drones in the queue at node $N _ { i d }$ with $R _ { n u m b }$ of recharging pads. We identify two types of congestion at the node-level.

## A. Phase 3: Spatial Proximity Analysis between Drone Pairs

The goal of this phase is to detect proximity-based interference between drone pairs. The input of this phase is the set of drone pairs from the previous phase that share the same segment within the same time interval. However, spatio-temporal overlap alone does not necessarily indicate interference, as drones may operate at different coordinates within the same segment and time. For each drone pair, we compute the separation between the drones along x, y, z axis using (5)â(7) (Lines 17-22). Moreover, there may be instances of collision where a drone pair shares the same coordinates simultaneously, resulting in zero separation along the $x , y , z { \mathrm { ~ a x e s } }$ (Lines 23- 24). Hence, a proximity-based interference event occurs if the spatial separation $D _ { s e p }$ between drone pair $d _ { i }$ and $d _ { j }$ along $x , y , z { \mathrm { ~ a x i s } }$ is greater than 0 but less than a certain separation threshold $( D _ { t h r e s h o l d } )$ (refer to (13)) (Lines 25-26). These thresholds vary along the 3D axes based on the spatial configuration of the drones (e.g., top-down, front-rear, side-by-side) within the segment. The output of this phase is the set of all drone services experiencing interference IDS and collision CDS in the time interval $\Delta t$ in segment S (Line 29).

## B. Segment-Level Interference Severity Assessment

The input of this module is the set of all drones experiencing proximity-based interference from the peer drones. This module assesses the overall severity level based on the cumulative impact of inter-drone interference events occurring in a segment. To quantify the overall severity level of interference, we first compute the impact of interference on each drone relative to its peer drones operating in the same segment. In this regard, we compute the impact of interference on the dronesâ battery consumption. We then assess how this updated battery consumption due to interference affects the expected delivery time of the drone. We use (17) from the model proposed in [24] to compute the expected battery consumption $( E X ( b _ { i } ) , S _ { k } )$ of the drone $d _ { i }$ as

it traverses segment $S _ { k }$

$$
E X ( b _ { i } , S _ { k } ) = \frac { d _ { i } ^ { S _ { k } } } { V _ { d _ { i } } ^ { S _ { k } } } \left( \frac { ( M _ { d _ { i } } + m _ { d _ { i } } ) \times V _ { d _ { i } } ^ { S _ { k } } } { \frac { 3 6 0 0 } { g } ( \eta r ) } + p w \right)\tag{17}
$$

where, $d _ { i } ^ { S _ { k } }$ is the distance traversed by the drone in the skyway segment, $V _ { d _ { i } } ^ { S _ { k } }$ is the speed of the drone, $M _ { d _ { i } }$ represents the mass of the drone, $m _ { d _ { i } }$ is the payload mass being carried. The gravitational acceleration constant is denoted by g, Î· refers to the motor/propeller power transfer efficiency, r represents the lift-to-drag ratio of the drone and pw is the power consumption of the droneâs electronics. The impact on battery consumption is determined based on the percentage change in expected battery consumption (EX[b]) of the drone i due to interference from the peer drone using (18).

## VI. EXPERIMENTATION AND RESULTS

In this section, we present the details of our dataset and discuss the results of each phase from our proposed framework. ## A. Dataset Description

1) Drone Delivery Dataset: In our experiments, we use a real-world skyway network dataset based on the 3D model of Sydney CBD [26]. This network consists of 30 nodes and 75 LoS-based skyway segments. The skyway segment serves as a delimited airspace for drones to transit between nodes. The dimensions of each segment are based on the compact size of the Crazyflie drones $( \mathrm { i . e . , 9 2 \times 9 2 \times 2 9 }$ mm) and the constraints of operating within an enclosed indoor environment due to government restrictions on outdoor drone testing. The length of each segment corresponds to the distance between the nodes, and the height is uniformly set to 100 cm to align with the vertical clearance of the testbed. The width of each segment ranges from a minimum of 10 cm to a maximum of 100 cm. The former is defined based on the width of the Crazyflie drone (i.e., 9.2 cm) for safe traversal of drones through the segment airspace. The latter is defined to prevent overlap with the drone traffic of nearby segments, which may lead to collisions or near-misses.

We use Crazyflie $2 . 1 ^ { 5 }$ as the drones operating in the skyway network due to their compact size and suitability for repetitive indoor experiments. The trajectory of each drone from the source to the destination node is based on $\mathbf { A } ^ { * } ( \mathbf { A } \mathrm { - s t a r } )$ algorithm to ensure the shortest path consisting of nodes and segments. Each dronesâ trajectory in a segment is a sequence of waypoints $W _ { 1 } , W _ { 2 } , . . , W _ { n }$ , where each $W _ { i }$ is defined by a tuple of 3D coordinates $< ( x _ { i } , y _ { i } , z _ { i } ) >$ , that the drone traverses at a specific timestamp. The recharging duration at the intermediate nodes is computed using (23) if the droneâs battery is insufficient to traverse the next segment. This change in battery consumption serves as the impact of inter-drone interference on the expected battery consumption of the drone. For the inter-drone interference dataset, we collected the impact on dronesâ battery power consumption for varying spatial configurations, i.e., top-down, front-rear, side-by-side, top-down offset, and side-by-side offset between drones in a 3D segment. We also conducted the experiments with varying inter-drone distances, i.e., 30 cm, 40 cm, 50 cm, 80 cm, and 100 cm, for each of the aforementioned spatial configurations. The experimental setup and dataset details are available at [9] and [25], respectively. 3) Relatability of Indoor Drone Testbed to Outdoor Settings: We setup an indoor drone testbed due to government restrictions on conducting outdoor experiments with commercial drones [28]. While the testbed replicates a scaled-down 3D model of Sydneyâs CBD, with the compact size Crazyflie as the drones operating in the network, it closely mimics the real-world settings [26]. The small-sized Crazyflie drones share the same multi-rotor architecture as the large-sized drones (e.g., DJI Flycart 30, Amazon MK30) used by companies like Amazon and DHL for package delivery [29]. Therefore, they exhibit similar aerodynamic behavior, which includes the generation of turbulent airflow due to continuous propeller rotation, which may impact the performance of nearby drones operating in close proximity [9]. However, the magnitude and spatial proximity at which drones may impact would scale up due to the magnitude of turbulence, which increases with the increase in the size of propellers [30]. Thus, the interference behavior observed in the testbed is relatable, as it captures the aerodynamic dynamics that are expected to scale with drone size in practical deployments.

Although the segment dimensions are modeled based on the small-size Crazyflie drones operating in the indoor settings, they would scale up given the large-size drones with a wingspan of 1980 mm expected to operate in urban skyway networks [31]. For instance, the length of the segment would correspond to the actual distance between the building rooftops (i.e., nodes), and the height would be defined based on the aviation authority regulations .6 The segment width would be proportional to the minimum of the width of the building rooftops, which serve as the endpoints of the segment. This would ensure that drones do not collide with the drones operating in the neighboring segments upon segment traversals between nodes. Albeit the expansion of segment dimensions, the skyway segments remain a delimited airspace given the complexity of skyway network infrastructure and predicted volume of drone traffic operations [32], [33]. Therefore, the indoor testbed closely mimics the delimited nature of the skyway segments where drones would follow a LoS path between nodes.

## C. Performance Evaluation

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed approach in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and computational time. We conducted experiments to compare the performance of our proposed heuristic-based interference detection approach against the baseline exhaustive and K-means clustering approach. Our baseline approach is an exhaustive approach that considers all possible drone pair combinations as a solution space to detect inter-drone interferences. We use the K-means clustering approach to perform spatio-temporal clustering of drone services based on the heuristics discussed in (14).

Effectiveness: We conduct a third experiment to evaluate the performance of our proposed approach considering the different numbers of drones operating in the network. The baseline approach exhaustively checks the spatio-temporal overlapping between all possible drone-pairs combinations. Therefore, we consider the interference detection results from the baseline exhaustive approach as ground truth values in these experiments. Fig. 13(a) and (c) show that our proposed time-based heuristic and K-means approaches have optimal accuracy and recall scores. In this context, recall measures the proportion of true interference events that the algorithm correctly detects out of all actual interference events. The results show that both approaches accurately identified all the inter-drone interference events in a segment (Fig. 13(a) and (c)). However, these approaches have a slightly low precision score of 88% in comparison to the baseline exhaustive approach (Fig. 13 (c)). Precision measures the proportion of correctly classified interference events out of all instances that the algorithm detected as interference. This precision score indicates that the proposed approach classifies some non-interference events as interference events. However, the proposed approach correctly detects all true inter-drone interference events (as evidenced by the 100% recall). It is crucial to detect all interference events accurately in scenarios where missing an inter-drone interference event could have a critical outcome with the cost of a few misclassifications. For instance, in scenarios where drones deliver medical packages, any undetected interference could delay essential deliveries, potentially risking lives.

Efficiency: In the fourth experiment, we evaluate the efficiency of our proposed approach in terms of computational time. We use the run-time efficiency metric to measure the algorithmâs computational speed. The run-time efficiency (RT E) is calculated as the following:

$$
R T E = { \frac { 1 } { E T } }\tag{26}
$$

where ET (execution time) is the number of drone pair instances processed per unit time. Fig. 14(a) shows that the proposed time-based heuristic approach outperforms both the baseline exhaustive and K-means approaches in terms of execution time. This is because these approaches differ significantly in defining their solution space consisting of drone pairs for detecting interference events. Defining solution space is critical as it directly influences the efficiency of the approach. It is computationally expensive to consider all possible peer-to-peer drones for detecting interferences. Therefore, both the proposed approach and the K-means approach make a set of clusters based on the heuristic defined in (14), followed by the peer-to-peer drone pairs within the same cluster using (15). We use the elbow method to determine the optimal number of clusters for defining the solution space in the K-means approach [34], [35]. As a result, the proposed approach makes a greater number of clusters in comparison to the K-means approach, as shown in Fig. 14(c). This is because K-means optimizes the clusters based on minimizing intra-cluster variance using numerical data (i.e., takeoff times) while treating categorical values (i.e., destination nodes) less granularly. Therefore, the proposed approach results in fewer peer-to-peer drone pairs for detecting interferences in comparison to K-means which leads to fewer but large-sized clusters as all the possible drone pair combinations are considered for each cluster (Fig. 14(b) and (c)). Therefore, the computational time required for spatio-temporal proximity analysis of drone pairs in each cluster is significantly reduced in the proposed approach compared to the K-means approach. This makes the proposed approach computationally efficient for time-sensitive transportation of medical supplies, where detecting interferences promptly ahead of time is crucial. Although both approaches perform equally effectively in detecting segment-level interference events. However, the proposed heuristic-based approach performs â 70% faster than the baseline exhaustive approach and â 48% faster than the K-means approach.

## VII. CONCLUSION

We propose a novel inter-drone interference detection framework as a prerequisite to ensure the resilient operation of drone services. The proposed framework aims to detect and assess the impact of inter-drone interference on the expected QoS requirements of drone services. The proposed framework considers clustering-based spatio-temporal comparison between drone pairs in a 3D space to detect inter-drone interferences. In addition, the proposed approach assesses the impact of interference on the dronesâ energy consumption. The experimental results reveal that the proposed clustering-based approach for interference detection significantly reduces the computational time while providing optimal results. Our future work will focus on resolving inter-drone interferences to maximize the satisfaction of drone providers using the skyway network.
