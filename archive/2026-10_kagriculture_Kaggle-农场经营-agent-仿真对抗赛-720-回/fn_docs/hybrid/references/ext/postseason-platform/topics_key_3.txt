A replay chassis, a stack of small layers, and a lesson about trusting the wrong numbers

First, thanks to the hosts. This is just my second competition so i am trying to adapt on how to write writeups. This one is different from most Kaggle competitions and that difference ended up being the whole story for me. There is no fixed test set here. You submit agents, they play each other on a live ladder, and the final rank comes from a Bradley-Terry fit over games played after the deadline. I didn't fully respect what that meant until late, and it cost me, so I'm going to be honest about that part too.

I'll say upfront that I didn't build an agent from scratch. The public "Shepherd's Ledger" tape-replay chassis was already the strongest thing anyone was sharing, and rewriting 25,000 lines to maybe match it felt like the wrong bet. So I took it as fixed and asked a smaller question instead: given a strong replay agent, what small edits actually move win rate in this economy? Every idea became one layer, bolted onto the agent, wrapping whatever was underneath, looking at the action it was about to take and changing only one specific thing. If an idea didn't survive measurement, I ripped the layer back out. That kept everything honest and testable.

Overview

The final agents are the chassis plus a stack of these layers. Picture it as an onion: the chassis sits at the bottom and makes a decision, then each layer gets a chance to tweak that decision on its way out (see the diagram below). The two I submitted were shepFOB4 (the full stack) and shepFOB3n (the same thing minus the last two layers).



The work really had three parts. First, understanding the market, which is what told me most of my "clever" ideas were actually hurting me. Second, the handful of layers that survived. Third, a test harness good enough to tell a real $30-per-game gain from pure noise. I'll walk through each, and then the mistake.

The one thing you have to understand about the market

The market never recovers inside a game. A product's price depends only on how much of it is currently sitting in the market, and that number only moves when someone trades or when the town eats a little on a fixed schedule. There's no "the price will bounce back tomorrow."

Once that clicked, a lot of my instincts turned out to be backwards. If I hold a lot back to sell it later at a better price, the price doesn't go up, I've just stepped aside and let my opponent sell into empty space. I tested this more times than I'd like to admit, and it was always the same answer: holding back helps the opponent two to four times more than it helps me. In this game, dumping is a weapon and patience is a gift to the other player.

Hi everyone,

First of all, I’d like to thank the organizers for such an interesting competition.

This is only my third Kaggle competition overall, and the first one that I approached seriously. I’m a former .NET developer with 20+ years of experience who decided to switch into AI/ML, and I’m trying to learn this new field with the help of AI itself.

I think Kaggle competitions are a great environment for trying yourself in something unfamiliar. In my opinion, AI models are an incredible multiplier of your capabilities. If you are unfamiliar with a topic, they can help you get up to speed very quickly. And if you already know the domain, they can increase your productivity and capabilities several times over.

Just like in my previous competitions, my workflow for this competition was built around Claude Code. In my experience, Claude Code and Anthropic models are especially good for different kinds of research. I also use Codex and OpenAI models, but my experience with them has been less pleasant so far.

I used Fable with high effort as the orchestrator, while Opus subagents were responsible for doing most of the actual work.

I’ll start with the main mistake I made: I did not build a proper system for tracking experiments and their results. I left that part mostly to the model, and eventually paid the price for it. From time to time, it felt like we were simply going in circles.

I also failed to establish a proper system for evaluating experiment results. Again, I trusted Fable to handle this. It repeatedly rejected experimental results because it decided they did not meet some very high criteria that it had set for itself.

By the time I noticed what was happening, I discovered that many useful changes to the agent had simply been discarded. Once we restored and applied those changes, we saw a significant jump in performance.

The model had a tendency to invent unclear or unnecessarily strict evaluation criteria fairly often.

Topic #744823: Suggestions for future simulation competitions: compute transparency and ladder slots
  Author: デワンシュ
  Posted: 2026-10-01 04:41:44.035000
  Votes: 10  Comments: 0

Thanks to the hosts for a fun and deep game. As a small team with a modest budget, here are two suggestions for future simulation competitions.




Compute transparency. The rules limit runtime but not offline compute, and approaches here ranged from a single GPU to large multi-core and multi-GPU setups. That's within the rules, but it's hard to tell how much of the final ranking reflects ideas versus budget. It would help if top teams voluntarily shared approximate training and evaluation compute in their write-ups. A compute-capped track, or separate recognition for one, could also be worth considering.


More room to iterate on the ladder. With only the latest two submissions active, each upload retires an older one along with all its games, so testing a new idea live costs the rating history of a proven agent. A third active slot, or a periodic re-rating of recent submissions, would make iteration less costly for teams that rely on the live ladder rather than large offline evaluation.


Looking forward to the write-ups, and congratulations to everyone who pushed this so far.

Topic #744808: Notes and reflections on agent capabilities
  Author: Michael Timbs
  Posted: 2026-10-01 02:01:20.906000
  Votes: 5  Comments: 0

In this competition I tried a full agent driven approach where I set the direction but offloaded all the work to the agent. I tried to only interface via either mobile chat or CLI and not read the or review the code too closely at all. 

I did not have high hopes for this approach because I heavily use these agents every day for my regular work and they are still very limited in abilities in that domain and you need to inspect output very closely. This competition seemed like a good test bed though because the stakes for "code quality" and correctness/performance are much lower and the black box approach of steering by visible outcomes seemed more viable. The complexity of this competition is also much lower than day to day work so it felt like there was a chance of success.

With the competition closed I can safely say that the experiment was a complete disaster. I thought the models would perform better here than they do in my regular use but it was not the case. At their worst the models (especially Opus) were so dense that I was suspecting they were detecting AI research use cases and routing to an adversarial model to deliberately sabotage efforts. I'm not talking about a weaker and less useful model, but rather an intelligent model who's goal was to introduce bugs and sabotage efforts (eg just ignore orders, tell me search was running when it wasn't etc). Even when things were going well the models were still very stupid, lacked any nuance or understanding and could not hold handle even moderate complexity in requirements. It was very much like working with the kind of person you manoeuvre to get off your team because they are not good to work with.

It's possible some people had more success by fully handing over the reigns and allowed some form of hill climbing to happen but for my approach of trying to use them as an assistant to carry out orders it was so very far from being useful. A talented/smart undergrad would run circles around these things.

I could write at great length about the failure modes throughout the last few months. They are numerous and varied and paint a picture of generic limitations across the board rather than specific weaknesses. I do not think I will try use agents again for a competition any time soon. Even Astra 6 and Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 are too far away from where they need to be, it's hard to quantify that distance but it is very large.

I'm glad to have had the opportunity to learn about reinforcement learning through this competition.   

A. Problem Definition: Reachable states within 720 turns



(1) Motivation &amp; World–Micro Architecture

Kaggriculture is an intractable 720-turn planning problem (30 days × 24 hours). Inspired by Rubik's Cube stage-solving and Pattern Databases (PDBs).

We operationalize this framework via a two-tier hierarchy:



World (Macro Strategy): "Which state to aim for?"
Compresses global farm states into strategic embeddings using patterns learned from user replays.

Micro (Atomic Execution): "How to get there?"
Fuses World's strategic context with real-time turn observations to execute worker movements, field tasks, and market trades.
